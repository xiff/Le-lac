Breathing in cold water: what happens, and what helps

By the My Lake team · published 15 November 2026

In short

In water below 15 °C, the body reacts to entry with a gasp reflex, then hyperventilation you cannot control, which can bring on panic. What is documented: get in slowly, stay where you can stand until your breathing settles, keep your head out of the water. We found no solid source for a specific breathing technique, so we do not recommend one.

What happens when you get into cold water?

The Outdoor Swimming Society describes it: below roughly 15 °C, getting in triggers “cold shock”, which starts with a sudden gasp reflex. It is followed by a period of hyperventilation that you cannot control: breathing becomes very rapid, which can feel like panic. New swimmers also tend to go upright in the water, where you are less buoyant, and panic can set in faster. A landmark review in Experimental Physiology counts these first reactions among the most hazardous of immersion.

What helps, according to the sources

  • Get in slowly: this is the advice of the Outdoor Swimming Society and of Helsana, which adds to wet your body gradually.
  • Stay where you can stand until the reflex has passed and your breathing is under control (Outdoor Swimming Society).
  • Keep your head out of the water: Helsana advises against putting it under.
  • Do not go alone: if your breathing runs away, someone must be able to react (Helsana).

What about breathing techniques?

Plenty of methods (slow breathing, breath-holds, cycles) circulate online. For winter swimming we found no scientific source or lifesaving body that recommends a specific one, and we do not repeat them. What is established is simpler: give the body time to get through the reflex before you swim. A swimmer interviewed by Helsana on Lake Geneva, in water at about ten degrees, says his breathing becomes slower and steadier in cold water; that is a personal account, not proof.

When to get out

As soon as you feel uncomfortable. The SSS rule sets the maximum time: as many minutes as the water has degrees, and far fewer at first. Afterwards: warm up properly.

Disclaimer. This article gives general information taken from the sources cited; it is not medical advice. Cold-water swimming puts real strain on the heart and can be dangerous: talk to a doctor before you start, especially with a heart condition, high blood pressure or during pregnancy. Never swim alone and follow lifeguards’ instructions. You swim at your own risk; in an emergency, call 144 in Switzerland or 112.
