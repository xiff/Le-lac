The seiches of Lake Geneva: the lake that oscillates

By the My Lake team · published 17 November 2026

In short

A seiche is an oscillation of a lake’s water: the level falls on one side while it rises on the other. On Lake Geneva, longitudinal seiches reach 30 centimetres at most, with a period of about 73 minutes. They are caused by wind or by a low-pressure system.

What is a seiche?

The word “seiche” was used around Lake Geneva for the visible oscillations of the lake, before it took on a precise meaning in physics. It covers two kinds of standing waves: those of the free surface, between air and water, and those of the thermocline, between surface water and deep water (see internal seiches).

How many centimetres, how many minutes?

For surface seiches, caused by wind or by a low, Wikipedia gives, for Lake Geneva, a maximum height of 30 centimetres for longitudinal seiches, with periods of about 73 minutes. Transverse seiches, between Morges and Évian, are much shorter.

Who studied them?

François-Alphonse Forel, Édouard Sarasin and Philippe Plantamour made the first studies, with instruments measuring the lake level at Vevey and Geneva. Forel distinguished longitudinal and transverse seiches, then three main types, and proposed a formula: the duration of half a seiche equals the length of the lake section divided by the square root of gravity times the mean depth.

And at the water’s edge?

A level that rises and falls a few centimetres over more than an hour is noticeable mostly on flat shores and jetties. Nothing alarming: it is the lake sloshing.
