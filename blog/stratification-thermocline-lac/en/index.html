The layers of a lake: epilimnion, thermocline, hypolimnion

By the My Lake team · published 19 November 2026

In short

In warm weather a lake sorts into three layers: the epilimnion (warm, at the surface), the thermocline (the zone of rapid thermal transition) and the hypolimnion (colder, down to the bottom). It is because warm water is less dense than cold water: it floats on top.

Why layers?

Most fluids have a density that falls as temperature rises: the warm part rises above the cold part. The surface water of a still lake is therefore warmer than the water a metre below.

The three layers

  • The epilimnion: the warm upper layer. Wind and waves circulate the water there, spreading heat roughly evenly over the first tens of metres.
  • The thermocline (or metalimnion): the middle layer, a zone of rapid thermal transition. It can change depth over the course of the day.
  • The hypolimnion: the colder layer, down to the bottom. In a eutrophic lake it can run short of oxygen.

Why it is useful to know

The temperature the app gives is the model’s at about 70 cm: the surface layer. Lower down, the water is colder. When the wind tilts the thermocline, the water can change quickly: see internal seiches. At the end of the year the layers mix: turnover.
