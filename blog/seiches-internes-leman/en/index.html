Why the water can suddenly turn cold: internal seiches

By the My Lake team · published 20 November 2026

In short

In summer the lake surface warms and a thermocline forms: a zone of rapid transition between surface and deep water. The wind can tilt that boundary: this is an internal seiche. On Lake Geneva, near the shores, its amplitude at depth can reach 10 metres, with movements of 35 cm/s for 5 to 6 hours (Wikipedia).

What happens in summer?

The warming of surface water creates, at an intermediate depth, a stable thermocline: the zone of rapid thermal transition between surface and deep water. The upper layer is called the epilimnion, the lower one the hypolimnion.

A seiche inside the lake

Wind blowing over the surface tilts this interface, which shows up as a standing wave, like those of surface seiches. Since the density difference is smaller than between air and water, these waves have a larger amplitude at depth; they reach the surface only much attenuated. Near the shores of Lake Geneva, the amplitude at depth can reach 10 m, associated for 5 to 6 hours with movements of 35 cm/s within the water.

What this may explain, and what we do not know

When the boundary between layers tilts toward a shore, water from another layer can come close: this is a plausible explanation for the sudden temperature drops swimmers report after a gust of wind. Wikipedia does not explicitly link internal seiches to these experiences, and we cannot say, beach by beach, when it happens.
